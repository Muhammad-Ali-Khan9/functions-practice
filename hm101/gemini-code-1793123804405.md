# Comprehensive Study Guide: Form and Function of Sentences & Run-On Sentences

## Detailed Topic Explanations

### 1. Functional Classification of Sentences
Sentences are functionally categorized by their purpose in communication[cite: 2]:
* **Declarative:** Makes a statement and ends with a period[cite: 2] (e.g., *"A lie told often enough becomes the truth."*[cite: 2]).
* **Interrogative:** Asks a direct question and ends with a question mark[cite: 2] (e.g., *"What is history but a fable agreed upon?"*[cite: 2]).
* **Imperative:** Expresses a command or request[cite: 2]. It usually uses an implied subject (`[You]`) and ends with a period[cite: 1, 2] (e.g., *"Get in your tanks."*[cite: 2]).
* **Exclamatory:** Expresses strong emotion or feeling and always ends with an exclamation mark[cite: 2] (e.g., *"Ouch! That hurts!"*[cite: 2]).

---

### 2. Structural Classification of Sentences
Sentences are structurally defined by the quantity and relationship of independent (main) and dependent (subordinate) clauses[cite: 2]:
* **Simple Sentence:** Contains exactly **one independent clause** (one subject-predicate dynamic) and no dependent clauses[cite: 2]. Simple sentences can still include compound subjects or compound predicates[cite: 2] (e.g., *"The prisoner and friend escaped but were later caught."*[cite: 2]).
* **Compound Sentence:** Contains **at least two independent clauses** and no dependent clauses, typically joined by a coordinating conjunction with a comma, or a semicolon[cite: 2].
* **Complex Sentence:** Contains **one independent clause** and **at least one dependent clause**[cite: 2]. A comma is used to separate the clauses when the dependent clause precedes the main clause[cite: 2] (e.g., *"When the cake is brown, remove it from the oven."*[cite: 2]).
* **Compound-Complex Sentence:** Contains **at least two independent clauses** and **at least one dependent clause**[cite: 2] (e.g., *"While Ali reads novels, Basit reads newspapers but Sara reads magazines."*[cite: 2]).

---

### 3. Clause Dynamics: Independent vs. Dependent
* **Clause:** A word group containing a subject and a verb[cite: 2].
* **Independent Clause:** Expresses a complete thought and can stand alone as a distinct sentence[cite: 2].
* **Dependent Clause:** Begins with a subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun, contains a subject and verb, but expresses an incomplete thought and cannot stand alone[cite: 2] (e.g., *"after he gave up chocolate"*[cite: 2]).

---

### 4. Run-On Sentences and Punctuation Errors
A run-on sentence occurs when two or more independent clauses are improperly joined without appropriate punctuation or conjunctions[cite: 2].

#### Common Types of Run-On Errors:
1. **Fused Sentences:** Mashing two main clauses together with no punctuation at all[cite: 2] (e.g., *"Akbar likes pets he has a beagle."*[cite: 2]).
2. **Comma Splices:** Incorrectly joining two independent clauses with only a comma instead of a coordinating conjunction or semicolon[cite: 2] (e.g., *"Jamila likes to cook, she makes chicken every day."*[cite: 2]).
3. **Polysyndeton / Overused Conjunctions:** Using more conjunctions than a sentence requires to string clauses together endlessly[cite: 2] (e.g., *"We went to the park and we ate dinner and we got ice cream and when it got dark we chased fireflies."*[cite: 2]).

#### Strategies for Repairing Run-On Sentences:
* **Option 1 (Period):** Split the independent clauses into separate sentences using a period[cite: 2].
* **Option 2 (Semicolon):** Join closely related independent clauses using a semicolon `;`[cite: 2].
* **Option 3 (Comma + Coordinating Conjunction):** Join clauses using a comma followed by a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS: *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*)[cite: 2].
* **Option 4 (Subordination):** Convert one of the independent clauses into a dependent clause using a subordinating conjunction (e.g., *because, although, since, while*)[cite: 2].

## Freshman-Level Advanced Questions & Answers

### Question 1
**Analyze the structural syntax of the following sentence: *"Although the database cluster crashed during peak hours, the backend engineering team restored the service within minutes, but the operations lead still ordered a complete post-mortem audit."* Identify its exact structural classification, count the total number of independent and dependent clauses, and map out each clause.**

* **Answer & Detailed Breakdown:**
  * **Structural Classification:** **Compound-Complex Sentence**[cite: 2].
  * **Clause Breakdown:**
    * **Dependent Clause:** *"Although the database cluster crashed during peak hours"* (Starts with the subordinating conjunction "Although", contains subject "database cluster" and verb "crashed", expresses incomplete thought)[cite: 2].
    * **Independent Clause 1:** *"the backend engineering team restored the service within minutes"* (Contains subject "backend engineering team" and predicate "restored...", expresses a complete thought)[cite: 2].
    * **Independent Clause 2:** *"the operations lead still ordered a complete post-mortem audit"* (Contains subject "operations lead" and predicate "ordered...", joined by coordinating conjunction "but")[cite: 2].
  * **Summary Count:** 1 Dependent Clause + 2 Independent Clauses[cite: 2].

### Question 2
**Deconstruct the following sentence for punctuation errors: *"The software architecture team refactored the legacy authentication codebase, however the QA team discovered multiple critical edge cases during load testing."* Identify the specific error type present, explain why it violates grammatical standards, and provide two distinct grammatically correct revisions.**

* **Answer & Detailed Breakdown:**
  * **Error Type:** **Comma Splice / Run-On Sentence**[cite: 2].
  * **Grammatical Violation:** The word *"however"* is a conjunctive adverb, not a coordinating conjunction[cite: 2]. Joining two independent clauses (*"The software architecture team refactored..."* and *"the QA team discovered..."*) using only a comma before a conjunctive adverb results in a comma splice error[cite: 2].
  * **Revision Method 1 (Semicolon + Conjunctive Adverb):**
    > *"The software architecture team refactored the legacy authentication codebase; however, the QA team discovered multiple critical edge cases during load testing."*[cite: 2]
  * **Revision Method 2 (Subordination):**
    > *"Although the software architecture team refactored the legacy authentication codebase, the QA team discovered multiple critical edge cases during load testing."*[cite: 2]

### Question 3
**Evaluate the sentence structure: *"The distributed database nodes sync state periodically and perform background garbage collection every midnight."* Is this a compound sentence or a simple sentence? Justify your decision by analyzing the subjects and predicates.**

* **Answer & Detailed Breakdown:**
  * **Classification:** **Simple Sentence**[cite: 2].
  * **Justification:** 
    * Although the sentence uses the coordinating conjunction **"and"**, it does **not** join two independent clauses[cite: 2]. 
    * It features a single complete subject (*"The distributed database nodes"*) performing two actions linked together (*"sync state periodically"* **and** *"perform background garbage collection every midnight"*)[cite: 2]. 
    * This forms a **compound predicate** attached to a single subject within **one single independent clause**, making it structurally simple, not compound[cite: 2].

### Question 4
**Identify the structural error in the following paragraph excerpt and explain how to resolve it using subordination: *"The developer optimized the SQL query execution plan it significantly reduced latency across all microservices."***

* **Answer & Detailed Breakdown:**
  * **Structural Error:** **Fused Sentence (Run-on Sentence)**[cite: 2]. Two complete independent clauses (*"The developer optimized the SQL query execution plan"* and *"it significantly reduced latency across all microservices"*) are joined together with zero punctuation or conjunctions[cite: 2].
  * **Resolution via Subordination:** Transform the first independent clause into a dependent clause by introducing a subordinating conjunction such as **"Because"** or **"Since"**[cite: 2]:
    > *"Because the developer optimized the SQL query execution plan, it significantly reduced latency across all microservices."*[cite: 2]
  * **Alternative Subordination:** 
    > *"The developer optimized the SQL query execution plan, which significantly reduced latency across all microservices."*[cite: 2]
